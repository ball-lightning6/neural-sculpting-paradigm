On 30-bit Mod3, a network must report the remainder of a binary integer divided by three using two output bits. With only the final remainder as a target, training loss fell but accuracy on unseen integers stayed near one in three. Teaching intermediate remainders helped the network learn. But with the unaided group stuck, how could we compare the number of examples each needed? The experiment moved to 14 and 16 bits, lengths both groups could learn.

At each length, the groups trained for 16,000 steps on the same inputs from the same initial weights, with three starts on each of two datasets. The auxiliary group also predicted prefix remainders: a leading `11` represents three, so its target is zero. Neither group received prefix answers at test time.

At 14 bits and 2,048 inputs, both groups got every training example right. Counting a test answer only when both output bits were right, mean accuracy over the final three checks rose from 80.16% to 99.14%. Runs reaching 99% at all three checks went from 0/6 to 5/6; the unaided group first reached 5/6 at 4,096 inputs. The separate 16-bit comparison at 6,000 inputs gave 61.38% versus 99.86%, with 0/6 versus 6/6 stable runs. The paired tables show a saving in distinct inputs at this budget, not an exact halving or less total supervision.
